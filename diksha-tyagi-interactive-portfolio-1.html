The rebuild

The headline idea: losing should be fun, not dead air. 75 people win. Thousands don't. Their version has nothing for that second group. Mine gives every non-winner an instant, funny, shareable "Almost Award" instead of silence. Repeat losses turn into their own little collection game.

Try it: lose the giveaway

This is a live demo of the mechanic. Click it a few times, the way a real repeat entrant would over the two weeks.

Theirs

Non-winners get nothing. The page and inbox both go quiet.

Mine

An instant, joke-titled "Almost Award" certificate, a new one each day, all shareable, all with a real small discount code baked in.

Because

Almost everyone who enters is losing. Give them something worth screenshotting instead of nothing at all, and the giveaway markets itself through the people who technically failed it.

Theirs

No reason to keep entering after you've lost once.

Mine

Badges are collectible. 3 in a row unlocks a guaranteed bonus entry the next day. 5 unlocks a real free-can code.

Because

It turns a losing streak into progress instead of a reason to give up and stop opening the emails.

And the smaller fixes underneath it

The badge idea is the headline. These are the supporting changes that make the rest of the funnel hold together.

Adding a badge mechanic on top of a sweepstakes can affect its legal structure (odds disclosures, "no purchase necessary" language). I'd flag that for legal review before building it.

The rest of the changes, and why

Give the closed state something to do

Theirs: come back at noon, plus a flavor list.
Mine: a live countdown, a "Remind me" option, and a "Find it near you" link.

Because the person on that page is the most motivated visitor you'll get. Give them a next step instead of a dead end.

Let the badges do double duty as a discount engine

Theirs: no purchase path for the people who don't win.
Mine: every Almost Award certificate carries a small, real discount code, not just a joke title.

Because the bit is more fun if it's not only a bit. It gives finance a reason to say yes to the idea.

Add a proof row

Theirs: flavor description only.
Mine: 5g sugar, high fiber, limited edition.

Because it ties the giveaway to the brand's "feel good soda" positioning, so the promo builds the brand as well as the flavor.

Lead with the party, not the trick-or-treaters

Theirs: hand the cans out to trick-or-treaters.
Mine: stock the party and make the porch everyone talks about.

Because it's a more believable use, and it keeps the fun.

Cross-link the two sweepstakes

Theirs: two sweepstakes running separately.
Mine: one line on each page pointing to the other.

Because shared traffic beats competing for the same attention.

    Something I've done

    A thought experiment, not a real campaign

    I built this one to show how I'd approach a brand from zero, not to claim it happened. If we talk, ask me about it and I'll tell you straight that it's a demo of process, the same way the Olipop work above is. Same format either way: the situation, what I did, and what happened, with the reason behind every decision.

    Operation: Achaar Empire

    Testing whether a food brand was worth starting, before starting it

    The situation. My grandmother makes a mango pickle that's been a family-only secret for two decades. Everyone who tastes it asks where to buy it. Nobody outside the family ever had. The real question wasn't "will people like it," it was "will strangers actually pay for it," and I didn't want her to buy jars and lids and spend a weekend cooking before we knew the answer.

    Move

    Built a one-page Instagram, no website, no shop, just a bio link to a Google Form.

    What it looked like

    "Coming soon: 48 hours only. 40 jars. First come, first served."

    Because

    I wanted the cheapest possible test. A form takes an hour to build; a store takes a weekend. If nobody filled it out, we'd know before spending a rupee on packaging.

    Move

    Wrote the launch post as a dare, not a sales pitch: "My grandmother won't post this. I'm doing it anyway."

    What it looked like

    Framed the pickle as something being smuggled out of the family, not something being sold.

    Because

    Nobody shares "new product." People share a story with tension in it. Scarcity plus a little rebellion is more shareable than a product shot.

    Move

    DM'd 15 small food accounts (2k to 10k followers) offering a free jar for an honest story, not a paid post.

    What it looked like

    No brief, no contract, just "try it, say what you actually think."

    Because

    At zero budget, borrowed trust beats paid reach. And an honest reaction from a small account travels further than a polished one from a big account nobody quite trusts.

    Move

    Capped it at 40 jars and said so on the page.

    What it looked like

    A visible counter: "23 of 40 claimed."

    Because

    A real cap is more convincing than a fake countdown timer. It also protects the one person actually doing the cooking.

    What I think would happen

    The form fills up inside the 48 hours, mostly from three of the small accounts, not the biggest one. The real bottleneck turns out to be my grandmother's patience, not demand. That's the actual finding: the idea was never short on customers, it was short on production capacity. So the next move isn't a bigger launch, it's a waitlist and a plan for how much she's actually willing to cook.

    What I'd do differently. Build the waitlist from day one instead of turning latecomers away with nothing. A "no" with no follow-up is a wasted contact.

    What working with me looks like

    I look at what's already running before I propose anything new. I fix the leaks before I spend on more traffic. And I write down why, so the team can argue with my reasoning instead of guessing at it.
